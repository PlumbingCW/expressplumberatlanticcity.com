Repiping in Atlantic City, NJ

Repiping replaces the water supply lines inside a home or small building when patching no longer keeps up. We scope the work in writing, phase it around people who live there, and give a clear price before work starts.

Signs your supply pipes are wearing out

One leak is a repair. A string of leaks in different spots is a pattern, and each patch can push stress onto the next weak section. Other signs show up at the tap. Pressure drops when two fixtures run, hot water is slow, or the water runs brown or orange before it clears.

  • Repeated pinhole leaks or wet spots in more than one room.
  • Low pressure at fixtures that is not explained by a closed valve.
  • Flaking, rust-colored or metallic-tasting water, mostly at first use in the morning.
  • Visible corrosion, crusty green or white buildup on joints.
  • Galvanized steel pipe, which is gray and magnetic, found in basements, crawlspaces or utility rooms.

If you have an active leak right now, shut the main valve and call about burst pipe repair. Repiping is planned work and is not the same as an emergency repair. If you are not sure where the water is going, leak detection can locate it before you decide.

Pipe materials compared

Copper, PEX, CPVC and galvanized at a glance
MaterialStrengthsWatch-outs
CopperLong track record, handles heat, rigid and neatNeeds soldered joints, costs more, can pit in some water
PEXFlexible, fewer fittings, quick to run through wallsMust be protected from sunlight, sharp bends and some chemicals
CPVCRigid plastic, lower cost, no corrosionCan turn brittle with age and heat
Galvanized steelWas common in older systemsRusts from inside, narrows over time, the main reason for replacement

No material is perfect for every building. We recommend one based on what is already in your walls, the water pressure, how many fixtures you have, and the budget. Ask what the fittings are made of, because mixed metals at a joint can corrode. Your water utility, the Atlantic City Municipal Utilities Authority, reports lead and copper results, and its 2023 samples were below action levels at all 30 sites tested.

That report covers the utility's side. Your own pipes are your side, and that is why a repipe starts with a look inside your building, not a guess from the street.

Repiping an occupied home or small building

Most people stay in their homes during a repipe, and a good plan makes that workable. We work in phases, so the water is off for set stretches and not for days at a time. We tell you in advance which sections will be shut, and we restore service at the end of each work period.

Phases of a repipe

  1. 1

    Survey and scope

    We trace the main, the risers, the branches and the fixtures, and write down what is included.

  2. 2

    Plan the order

    We pick a sequence, often starting with the kitchen and bath lines, so you keep some water.

  3. 3

    Open access

    We use existing closets, basements and small wall openings where we can, to keep cutting to a minimum.

  4. 4

    Run new lines

    New pipe is run, connected to fixtures and shutoffs, and the old lines are capped or removed.

  5. 5

    Pressure test

    The new system is filled and tested for leaks before any wall is closed.

  6. 6

    Close and patch

    Access openings are closed and patched to a rough or paint-ready state, with the finish work spelled out in the scope.

Rowhouses, condos and shared walls

In rowhouses, condos and apartment buildings, your pipes may share a wall, a chase or a riser with a neighbor. Ask your property manager or association who owns each section and whether the riser can be shut for a few hours. Your building may have rules about work hours, elevator use and notice to neighbors.

We work only on the lines we are hired to replace. A shared riser is a building decision, and we help you figure out what to ask. The same applies to the water service line that brings water from the street, which is a separate scope.

Spot repairs versus a full repipe

A repipe is a big job, and sometimes a smaller fix is the right call. If leaks are in one section, or the problem is a single failed fitting, replacing that run may be enough. We tell you when that is the case. If the pipe is galvanized and failing from within, patching tends to chase the next leak.

Choosing the scope
SituationLikely path
One leak, rest of the pipe in good shapeSpot repair
Several leaks in one wing or floorReplace that section
Galvanized pipe with low pressure and discolored waterFull or phased repipe
Remodel with walls already openRepipe while access is easy

Get the scope in writing before work begins: which lines, which fixtures, which walls get opened, who patches and paints, and whether a permit is expected. Permit rules vary by job, so ask your local construction office. New work is also a good moment to look at plumbing installation upgrades like shutoffs at each fixture.

What affects the cost of repiping

Size of the system

The number of fixtures, floors and feet of pipe sets most of the job.

Pipe material

Copper, PEX and CPVC differ in cost and in labor.

Wall and ceiling access

Finished walls and tight chases take longer than open framing.

Patching and finish

Whether drywall, tile or plaster repair is in the scope or left to others.

Permit needs

If your construction office asks for a permit or inspection, that time is part of the plan.

Replacing worn pipe is typically maintenance and not an insured event. Ask your insurer before you decide, and ask whether a sudden pipe failure would be handled differently.
