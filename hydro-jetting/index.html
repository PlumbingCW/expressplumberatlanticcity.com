How a hydro jet works

A jetter pumps water through a hose that ends in a nozzle with several small openings. Some jets point backward and pull the hose forward through the pipe. Others point outward and spin or sweep the pipe wall. The result is a scrubbing action across the full inside surface, not just a hole through the middle of the clog.

Pressure and flow both matter. Jetters run at pressures in the thousands of psi at the pump, but that figure varies by machine and job, and a careful operator dials it back for old or thin pipe. The nozzle shape matters as much as the pressure: a penetrating nozzle opens a blockage, while a wide-fan nozzle cleans the walls.

Snaked, and it clogged again

This is the call we hear most often. A cable passes through, water drains, and within weeks the same drain slows again. That pattern usually means the pipe wall is coated or narrowed, so the cable only cleared a channel through a still-restricted line. For ordinary single-fixture clogs, our drain cleaning page covers the cable work. Jetting is what follows when the pattern repeats.

  • The cable got stuck or came back wrapped in roots, which suggests roots entering at a joint.
  • A kitchen or restaurant line fills with grease every month or two.
  • Water drains slowly in several fixtures even after cabling.
  • A camera shows scale or hard deposits narrowing the pipe.
  • The line is long, with several bends, and a cable cannot clean the far end.

When a sewer backup cannot wait

When sewage or gray water is rising, the goal is to restore flow first and decide on a longer-term fix after. For a full building backup, the first move is often a main line cable, and jetting follows when the line is still restricted. Our sewer line cleaning page explains the backup case in detail.

We take after-hours jetting calls on the emergency line, and same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows. A late-night call still begins with a camera or access check, because blasting water into a cracked pipe can send debris and water into the soil around it.

Worth knowing

When jetting is the wrong tool

Jetting cleans a pipe that is intact. It does not repair one. A collapsed section, a large offset or a cracked clay pipe can fail or leak further under high pressure, and water may escape into the soil.

That is why a camera comes first when the line is old or the history is unknown. If the picture shows damage, we explain the repair route on our sewer line repair page rather than jetting through a break.

Older pipe and what we adjust

As general trade knowledge, older cast iron develops a rough, scaled interior, and old clay or fiber lines can have loose joints. We do not claim to know what pipe lies under any given Atlantic City street. We look first, then set the nozzle and pressure to suit what the camera shows. On thin or corroded pipe, a lower setting and a slower pass are the safer path.

If the pipe is sound but rough, some owners later ask about a liner, an epoxy coating applied inside the cleaned pipe. A line must be thoroughly cleaned before lining, so jetting is often the first stage. Whether lining suits your pipe is a separate decision, explained under repair options.

Getting ready, running the job, cleaning up

  1. 1

    Clear the work area

    Camera look

  3. 3

    Set up the jetter

    The hose goes in through the cleanout. We select a nozzle and pressure to suit the pipe and blockage.

    We pull the hose back slowly, scouring the wall, and flush loosened debris toward the main.
