Sump Pump Installation in Atlantic City, NJ

A sump pump installation gives water that collects in a low spot a place to go before it reaches your floor. In Atlantic City, where tides and storms push water around, the choices that matter are the pit, the pump type, the backup power and the discharge.

Why water behaves differently on this island

Atlantic City sits at about 7 feet of elevation on Absecon Island, with the ocean on one side and back-bay water on the other. The National Weather Service in Mount Holly sets tidal flood levels at the Atlantic City gauge, measured in feet MLLW. Those thresholds are a useful yardstick for deciding how hard your pump will be asked to work.

Tide gauge levels and what they mean for a pump
Gauge readingNWS descriptionWhat to do with your pump
At 6.0 ftMinor tidal floodingCheck that the float moves freely and the discharge line is clear
At 7.0 ftModerate, widespread roadway floodingTest the pump and the backup battery, and clear the pit of debris
At 8.0 ftMajor, structural damage possibleExpect long runtimes, keep a second pump or backup ready, and stay out of flooded rooms

A Rutgers study from 2019 projects 17 to 75 days per year of high-tide flooding by 2030, compared with less than once a year in 1950. For a homeowner, that means a pump is no longer an occasional storm tool. It may run on ordinary high tides, so it needs to be sized, powered and tested like equipment that gets used.

We do not assume what is below your building. Buildings differ: it might be a basement, a crawlspace, a low utility room or garage, or none of these. A sump pump belongs only where water actually collects, which a plumber confirms on site.

Sump pit or sewage ejector

These two get mixed up often. A sump pit collects clear groundwater or seepage and sends it outside. A sewage ejector pit collects wastewater from a below-grade bathroom, laundry or floor drain and lifts it up to the sewer line. They use different pumps, different covers and different venting.

Sump pump compared with sewage ejector
FeatureSump pumpSewage ejector
What it handlesGroundwater and seepageToilet, sink and laundry wastewater
Where it dischargesOutdoors, away from the foundationTo the building's sewer line
Pit coverLoose or vented lidSealed and vented to the roof or stack
SolidsSmall grit onlyPasses solids per the pump rating
Needs a plumberFor discharge and check valve workYes, with code-compliant venting

Never send toilet water to a sump pit, and never run a sump pump discharge into a sewer line without asking first. If you are unsure which you have, our sump pump repair page explains the failures we see, and a plumber can read the setup on a short visit.

What a sump pump installation actually involves

From dry floor to working pit

  1. 1

    Choose the low point

    The pit goes where water naturally collects, not where it is easiest to dig.

  2. 2

    Open the floor

    A section of slab is cut, soil removed, and a basin set so its rim sits flush with the floor.

  3. 3

    Set the pump

    The pump sits on a stable base in the basin with the float clear of the walls and discharge pipe.

  4. 4

    Add a check valve

    A check valve in the discharge line stops pumped water from falling back into the pit.

  5. 5

    Run the discharge

    The pipe goes outdoors to a spot that sheds water away from foundations and neighbors.

  6. 6

    Test under load

    Water is poured into the pit until the float lifts and the pump clears it, then the cycle is repeated.

Battery backup and storm-night power

The worst nights for flooding are often the nights the power flickers. A pump that stops when Atlantic City Electric goes out is not much of a defense. A battery backup pump, or a battery system that takes over the primary pump, keeps water moving during an outage.

Batteries need attention. Check the charger light, test the pump every few months, and replace the battery on the schedule its maker gives. A backup that has not been tested is a hope, not a plan.

Local note

What past storms tell you about the pit

Hurricane Sandy peaked at 8.8 ft at the Atlantic City gauge on October 29, 2012, above the 8.0 ft major flood level. New Jersey figures cited in news coverage say 9 percent of Atlantic City homes were seriously damaged, and that streets in Chelsea Heights still flood often.

The same coverage notes that water can rise through storm sewers during full-moon high tides and storms. A sump pump cannot stop that, because it only handles water that reaches your pit. Treat it as one layer, alongside raising stored items and knowing where your shutoffs are.

Where the discharge can go

Discharge rules differ from one municipality to the next, so ask your local construction office and, for sewer questions, ACMUA at (609) 345-3315 before the pipe is run. Many places do not allow groundwater to enter the sanitary sewer, and some limit discharge across sidewalks or onto neighboring property.

Whatever the rules say, a few practical points hold. Point the outlet away from the foundation, keep it above the level where storm water backs up, and avoid pipe runs that can freeze and block. Outlets that end near a storm drain can fill backwards when the drain is full, so ask what happens at a high tide.

  • Confirm that the spot you want to protect really collects water, rather than assuming it does.
  • Ask the local construction office what permit or inspection applies to a new pump and discharge.
  • Call 811 before digging a trench for a buried discharge line.
  • Have the pit sized for your pump with room for the float to swing freely.
  • Keep electrical outlets for the pump on a ground-fault circuit and off the floor.
  • Turn the power off at the breaker before touching any wet equipment, and stay out of standing water that may be near live wiring.
