Can a Tankless Water Heater Freeze in Atlantic City, NJ?

Yes, a tankless water heater can freeze in Atlantic City, NJ. Units in garages, crawl spaces and outdoor locations are most at risk, and a power outage in a cold snap removes the built-in protection. Here is how to lower the odds.

The honest answer about freezing

Tankless units hold only a small amount of water, but that water sits in a heat exchanger, valves and thin pipes that ice can split. Most models include freeze protection, usually small heaters or a routine that cycles the burner. That protection depends on power, and sometimes on gas flow.

A unit in a heated room with a sealed vent is rarely at risk. One in an unheated space, or one that loses power during a storm, can freeze. Our tankless water heater work includes checking where the unit sits and how it is protected.

What the climate numbers say

Local note

Local note: Atlantic City cold

NOAA climate normals for Atlantic City International Airport show a record low of -11 F, and seasonal snowfall from November through April typically runs 12 to 18 in.

What that means for you: the record low shows that deep cold is possible here, even if most winters are milder. If your unit is in a garage, outdoor closet or crawl space, plan for the cold extreme rather than the average winter.

Snow adds another issue. Heavy snow can bury outdoor vent terminations or drift over wall-mounted units, blocking exhaust and intake air. Clear snow from vents and outdoor units after every storm.

Where a tankless unit is most exposed

Look at your own setup honestly. If you can feel a draft where the pipes enter the wall, or the room gets as cold as the outdoors on a winter night, treat that as a warning sign. Moving the unit is a larger job, but sealing the gaps and insulating the lines is cheap and works well.

Pipes feeding the unit freeze first, in many cases. The heater itself may be fine while the supply line behind it bursts. Insulating the lines and sealing air leaks around them matters as much as the unit.

Why a power outage matters

Most freeze protection runs on electricity. If a storm knocks out power, the small heaters and recirculation features stop. Even gas units need power for the fan, igniter and controls. A short outage is usually fine. A multi-day outage in a deep freeze is when units fail.

Atlantic City Electric supplies power here. Know your plan before a storm: shut off the water to the heater and drain it if the building will go unheated for a long time.

Think about the longest outage you would tolerate. If the house loses power for a full day in a deep freeze, the room around the unit cools steadily, and the built-in protection that depends on electricity stops. Draining the unit before you leave on a winter trip, or before a storm that threatens extended outages, takes only a few minutes.

A small generator or battery backup can keep a unit's controls alive, but have it installed by a licensed professional. Running a generator indoors or in a garage creates a carbon monoxide hazard, so keep it well outside.

Steps that reduce the risk

  1. Keep the unit powered and the gas supply on during cold weather. Do not unplug it to save energy.
  2. Insulate hot and cold lines near the unit, and seal gaps in the wall or floor that let cold air in.
  3. Clear snow and ice from vent terminations and outdoor units after each storm.
  4. Leave a faucet dripping slightly during a hard freeze if the supply line runs through an exposed area.
  5. Before a long absence in winter, shut off the supply and drain the unit and lines.
  6. Call a plumber if the unit shows a fault code, no hot water or a leak after a cold night.

What to check if you think it froze

  • No hot water and a cold exterior wall near the unit, which suggests frozen supply lines.
  • A display fault code, which you can look up in the manual or read to the technician.
  • Wet insulation, drips or ice on the unit or nearby pipes.
  • Water pressure at the faucets, and whether cold water works normally.
  • Any popping or hissing sounds that might point to a split pipe.

If you see water or ice, close the supply valve to the heater. Do not use a torch or open flame to thaw pipes. If the pipe has split, our burst pipe repair team can stop the leak and repair the line.

Thawing is slow work for a reason. Warming a frozen pipe quickly can let split sections leak the moment the ice melts. Open a nearby faucet so melting water has somewhere to go, and keep the supply to the unit closed until a plumber has checked for cracks.

Frozen tankless versus a frozen tank heater

A tank heater holds dozens of gallons, so it resists freezing, but its pipes and valves still can. A tankless unit has little stored water, which means ice can form quickly in exposed parts. Neither design is immune. What matters is where it sits and how well it is protected.

Tankless units also have vent and condensate lines. Condensing models drain acidic condensate through a small tube, and a frozen tube can shut the unit down. That is a common winter call and an easy one to miss.
